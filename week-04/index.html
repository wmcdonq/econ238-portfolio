Storm deaths fell as the U.S. grew

The U.S. has more people than it did in the 1940s. Many people have also moved to states that get strong storms. Still, the death rate from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes is much lower now.

Walter McDonaldUniversity of RochesterData through 2023

A storm is a hazard. People and buildings in its path are exposed to it. Forecasts, safe buildings, shelters, roads, power, and health care can limit the harm. That is why a strong storm does not always kill more people. It is also why a lower death rate does not mean storms are safe.

What I did: I added U.S. deaths from the three hazards for each year in the National Weather Service record. I found each decade's yearly average. I divided that by the decade's average U.S. population. The 2020-23 bar is only four years, so it is shown in a lighter color.

This may be a sign that people learned how to stay safer. Forecasts got better. Buildings got safer. Warnings can reach people faster. Rescue teams and health care also got better. These things cost money. They also need rules and public services that work.

This is a clue that some people accept more storm risk. They may care more about a job, warm weather, family, taxes, or housing costs.

Be careful: A move into a state is not the same as a move into a floodplain or hurricane zone. This chart cannot prove why people moved.

Risk is more than weather

The IPCC found that more people and more property in exposed places were the main cause of long-run growth in disaster losses. After adjusting for population and wealth, the report did not tie the long-run rise in losses to climate change, but it did not rule out a climate role.

People choose a whole package. A coastal home may be close to a job or family. It may also have a view and a chance of flooding.

Policy should make danger and cost clear. It should not make other people pay for a risk they did not choose.

The temperature numbers are different

The Burke team looks at deaths linked to normal hot and cold days. They do not only count people listed as storm deaths by the National Weather Service. The team estimates that temperature may play a part in 5% to 12% of all deaths in the countries they study. They also find that cold kills more people than heat in every place they study.

Do not mix the measures. NWS numbers are direct reports tied to named weather hazards. The Burke numbers are estimates based on how death rates change when temperatures change.

The paper says many heat plans do not have strong proof behind them. Home weatherization is one example. Some programs cut hospital visits. Their energy savings were often smaller than expected. We need to ask what a program costs, who pays, and if the same money could save more lives another way.

But wait

The lower death rate does not prove that every safety program worked. The numbers do not tell us what would have happened without warming. They also leave out injuries, lost homes, stress, and money lost. The NWS may list hurricane deaths under wind, flood, or tornado. Records may also be better in recent years.

My claim is limited. The U.S. has gotten much better at keeping people alive during these three storm hazards. This happened even as more people moved to risky places. That is real progress, but there is still more work to do.

The storm matters, but so do our choices about where we live, what we build, and how we prepare.
