1. A warning from Oregon

On October twelfth, 2017, Oregon's Department of Environmental Quality issued an awkward warning. China's new import restrictions were disrupting the state's recycling markets. Sorting facilities were slowing down to remove unwanted material. Collections were still arriving. Storage space was limited. Some material collected for recycling might have to be disposed of instead. [1]

Think about what that meant for someone doing exactly what the recycling program asked. They could put the right items in the right cart and still have no control over whether a factory would use them. The act at the curb had stayed the same. Something on the other side of the world had changed.

The warning also asked residents to stop putting unaccepted items in recycling bins just because they hoped those items could be recycled. Officials called that wishful recycling. Good intentions were adding work at a time when the sorting plants could least afford it. [1]

There is an easy story to tell here. Americans were fooled. Recycling was a show. China stopped accepting the rubbish, and the show fell apart. That story has an appealing villain and a clear ending. It also skips some important evidence.

A different easy story says that households simply needed better instructions. If everyone rinsed containers and followed the rules, the problem would go away. That puts too much weight on the person standing over the kitchen bin. A household cannot create a paper mill or order a manufacturer to buy used plastic.

Oregon gives us a way to look more closely. Its experience joins a local environmental tradition to international trade. It also shows how several things we call recycling can perform very differently. A deposit bottle, a mixed paper bale, and a pile of assorted plastic are not the same product.

The question running through this lecture is whether a community has built a useful supply system or mostly built a collection system. Those can look the same from a driveway. The difference becomes visible when the buyer disappears.

To understand why that was such a shock in 2017, we need to go back to a time when putting a price on an empty container was a new political idea.

2. Five cents and a public promise

In 1971, Oregon passed the first statewide bottle-deposit law in the United States. Governor Tom McCall signed it on July second. According to the state's history, it faced strong opposition from beverage and container interests. Its aims included reducing litter and conserving resources. It initially covered beer and carbonated soft drinks. [2]

That is a smaller and more concrete promise than saving the planet through everything in your recycling cart. An empty drink container had become a public nuisance. The law gave people a reason to bring it back.

The program began in October 1972 with a five-cent refund. The Oregon Beverage Recycling Cooperative's history describes retailers taking returns and beverage distributors collecting the empties. The system connected the money paid at purchase to the route taken by the used container. It put responsibilities on businesses as well as customers. [3]

A deposit changes a small decision. Imagine finishing a drink while walking home. Carrying the empty bottle is inconvenient. Throwing it away is easier. A refund gives that inconvenience a price. Someone who does not care much about litter may still care about getting the money back. Someone else may collect a container the first person leaves behind.

This does not make the return system free. Someone has to take the bottles, store them, move them, and account for the money. The deposit makes participation more attractive. It does not erase those costs. A serious comparison would count them alongside cleaner streets and recovered material.

The law also put a practical habit inside a public story about Oregon. Protecting a place could mean doing something as ordinary as bringing a bottle to a store. There was a clear object, a clear return, and a fairly clear next step. That made the habit easier to understand than a distant set of environmental claims.

At roughly the same time, another image offered a broader promise. The Museum of Modern Art dates Gary Anderson's recycling symbol to 1970. Three arrows turn back toward one another. It is a powerful picture because it seems to finish its own story. Material comes around again. Nothing is left outside the loop. [4]

But a picture has no bill for sorting. It has no factory manager rejecting a load. It cannot show whether making something from used material costs more than making it from new material. We have to supply those missing parts ourselves.

Here is the distinction worth keeping in mind. A person returning a deposit bottle is responding to a designed set of payments and collection arrangements. A person seeing the arrows may take them as a general promise about what will happen next. The second promise can be much larger than the actual system behind it.

By 2017, the question was no longer just how to persuade households to participate. It was who would do the difficult work after they did.

3. A buyer across the Pacific

China became a major destination for material collected abroad. A 2018 study by Amy Brooks, Shunli Wang, and Jenna Jambeck found that China had taken about forty-five percent of the world's cumulative plastic-waste imports from 1992 through 2016. [5]

That number needs a careful label. It refers to internationally traded plastic waste over those years. It does not mean forty-five percent of every plastic item made in the world went to China. It does not tell us that every imported load was recycled successfully. Trade records show movement across borders, not a complete account of each object's final use.

Still, the figure shows how much the trade depended on one destination. Losing a buyer with that role would be different from losing one small customer among hundreds of equal customers.

Oregon's environmental agency explains the appeal of the trade in practical terms. Chinese manufacturing created demand for material. Labor and shipping costs, along with prices buyers offered, helped make export attractive. Oregon had also lost newsprint mills in Newberg and Oregon City by 2015, reducing nearby outlets for recovered paper. [6]

It is tempting to ask why anyone would send a low-value used item so far. Distance alone does not answer that question. A factory nearby that cannot use the material is not an alternative buyer. A farther factory may have the equipment, workers, and production plans needed to turn it into something saleable.

Consider a made-up example. A local processor can turn a particular load into usable material for two hundred dollars. A more distant processor can do it for eighty dollars, and shipping adds sixty. The distant option is cheaper even though the trip is longer. Those numbers are only an illustration. The point is that the full set of costs matters.

Now add something the invoice might leave out. Suppose the cheaper processor releases dirty water that harms people nearby. If those people are not paid for the harm, the low price does not prove the arrangement is good for society. It shows that the buyer and seller found terms they would accept. People outside the deal may have received a different bargain.

Economists call those unpaid effects external costs. Here the term asks a very ordinary question: who has to live with the mess, and did the price include their loss?

This makes the recycling trade difficult to judge with a single slogan. Trade can connect useful material to a factory that needs it. It can also move unpleasant work toward people with less power to reject it. Both possibilities require us to look beyond the port and into the work itself.

It also matters who can change the arrangement. A town may own trucks and have a contract with a sorting plant. That gives it some control over collection. It does not give it control over another country's import rules. A local service can quietly depend on decisions made far outside the local election.

That dependence stayed out of the household's daily view. People saw the cart emptied. They did not usually see a buyer deciding what a mixed load was worth.

This creates a problem of information as well as a problem of cost. A resident can check whether a truck arrived. Checking where a bale finally went is harder. It may pass through several businesses before reaching a manufacturer. A program can therefore be judged by its most visible activity even when its least visible activity determines much of the result.

That does not mean the people running collection programs were all dishonest. It means their reports need to answer the question residents actually care about. Tons collected and tons used are different measures. If the first number is easy to count and the second is hard, the easy number can take over the conversation.

4. People inside the loop

The documentary Plastic China, directed by Jiu-Liang Wang, brings people into this otherwise distant part of the story. Its producer's account describes an eleven-year-old girl, Yi-Jie, whose family lives and works at a small plastics workshop. She is not in school. Discarded English learning cards and broken dolls become part of her contact with a wider world. [7]

The same account describes Kun, the workshop owner, working toward the status that a car would bring. These details come from the film's official synopsis, not from an interview conducted for this lecture. The film appeared in the 2017 Sundance selection. It should not be treated as a survey of every recycling business in China. [7]

What makes those people important to this history is that they have aims of their own. A child wants education. A workshop owner wants a better position in life. They are not simply scenery behind an American environmental habit. The work provides income while also exposing families to costs that a customer far away may never see.

That raises a hard question. If a harmful workshop closes, what happens to the workers? Ending the harm matters. Losing the income matters too. It would be careless to assume that keeping a bad job is always best. It would also be careless to assume that closing it automatically gives the family a better one.

The right comparison depends on the available alternatives. Can production move to a cleaner facility? Can workers move with it? Who pays for better equipment? A ban can stop a stream of imports quickly. Building a different source of income is a separate task.

Chinese officials were making their own judgment about the trade. In a November 2017 account, the environmental ministry described Minister Li Ganjie's call for strict import controls and action against highly polluting processors. The policy was tied to the government's wider idea of ecological civilization. Restricting foreign waste was presented as part of improving China's environment and changing its development path. [8]

That is an official account of the government's position. It is not independent proof that every enforcement action achieved its goal. But it helps explain why Americans could not safely assume that China would keep accepting the same loads indefinitely.

An importing country can decide that an activity no longer fits the future it wants. Its willingness to take other countries' material is not a permanent public service owed to those countries. Once that willingness changes, exporters have to face costs they had been able to handle elsewhere.

The film and the policy belong in the same broad history, but we should not turn their timing into a claim that one caused the other. The sources used here do not establish that the documentary caused the import restrictions. The useful connection is the question both bring into view: what did this trade ask people in China to accept?

Oregon was about to encounter that question through prices, storage space, and rejected outlets.

5. The sorting line slows down

China's restrictions did not appear from nowhere. Oregon's account traces an earlier tightening to the Green Fence effort in 2013. The 2017 measures and the restrictions that followed in 2018 sharply narrowed access for mixed paper and post-consumer plastics. China also imposed a very low contamination limit on certain material it still allowed. [6]

We should separate a ban on a material from a higher standard for an allowed material. One says the shipment cannot enter in that category. The other says the shipment must be much cleaner. Those rules can create different problems, even if both make an old business arrangement harder to continue.

A sorting plant does not receive clean products in separate delivery boxes. Its job is to pull useful material from a mixed flow. That job has limits. Machines and workers can handle only so much in an hour. When the buyer demands a cleaner output, the plant may need another pass, more workers, different equipment, or a slower line.

Here is another simple illustration, not a measurement from an Oregon plant. Suppose a line usually takes ten loads in a day. Getting them clean enough now takes twice as long. Without a change in equipment or staffing, daily capacity falls to five loads. If ten still arrive, the plant gains a five-load backlog every day. Asking the public to recycle more does not solve that particular problem.

Storage buys time, but storage also has a limit. Nor can every operator afford to buy equipment before knowing whether a new buyer will sign a contract. A quick change in demand can hit a system whose expensive physical parts change slowly.

The October 2017 Oregon warning showed this problem before the full restrictions took effect. Incoming material, slower sorting, and limited space were no longer fitting together. The agency warned that disposal might sometimes become necessary. That was the uncomfortable point where the public promise met the physical limits of the system.

Notice what happened to bargaining power. If several buyers want your clean material, you can ask them to compete for it. If most of them disappear, the remaining buyer can be more selective. Material you hoped to sell may become material you have to pay someone to take. The same truckload can move from the revenue side of a business to the cost side.

There is no mystery in that change once we remember that a used material is valuable because of what someone can do with it. A label on the collection cart does not establish its sale price.

Nor does extra effort at one stage guarantee a benefit for the whole system. Imagine spending another hundred dollars sorting a load. That could be worthwhile if it produces enough usable material and avoids enough harm. It could be wasteful if there is still no use for the result. We need to compare the added benefit with the added cost, not just admire the effort.

For local officials, this created an ugly choice. Narrow the list and risk looking as if the community was retreating from recycling. Keep collecting and pay more while searching for outlets. Allow disposal and risk destroying the trust that helped residents participate in the first place.

There was no costless answer hidden behind a better slogan. The question was which costs the community would accept, who would pay them, and what the spending would actually achieve.

Trust made the choice harder. Consider a resident who learns that a collected load was sent to disposal. They may stop separating everything, including items that still have good outlets. An honest report about a specific failure could then weaken a useful part of the program. Hiding the failure could create a larger loss of trust later. Clear explanations need to say which materials are affected and why. Calling everything a success or everything a scam gives people very little help with the next item in their hands.

6. Two results in the same state

The claim that all recycling failed runs into Oregon's own 2018 report. The state reported recovering forty-point-eight percent of its waste, compared with forty-one-point-six percent in 2017. Recovery here is a broad category that includes more than recycling plastic. Those figures are not the percentage of plastic bottles turned into new bottles. [9]

More revealing is the difference inside the report. Oregon's total plastics recovery fell during the market disruption. Yet recovery of plastic, glass, and aluminum containers covered by the bottle law increased. The report connects that change with the higher deposit and the expansion of covered beverages. [9]

The deposit had risen from five to ten cents in April 2017. More beverage categories entered the system in 2018. The industry's cooperative reports that the return rate rose from sixty-four percent in 2016 to eighty-one percent in 2018. That is a reported before-and-after pattern, not a controlled experiment proving that the deposit alone caused every bit of the increase. [3]

Still, the contrast is useful. Oregon did not have one recycling machine that either worked or broke. It had different ways of gathering different materials, with different payments and outlets. A broad claim about the blue cart could miss what was happening through bottle returns.

The United States Environmental Protection Agency's 2018 figures make a related point. It estimated an eight-point-seven percent recycling rate for plastics overall. For plastic bottles and jars made from PET, the estimate was about twenty-nine percent. These are historical 2018 figures, not a claim about today's rates. They measure different groups of products. [10]

If we use the overall number to dismiss every bottle program, we ignore that difference. If we use the bottle number to praise all plastic packaging, we make the opposite mistake. The name of the material is not enough. Product design, collection, and a usable market all matter.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology points to practical needs such as separating plastic types, removing contamination at lower cost, and giving people reliable ways to compare recyclability. It also emphasizes that households setting products aside cannot complete recycling on their own. Manufacturers must use the recovered material. [11]

Those needs suggest a different way to judge a program. Instead of asking how much passed through the collection truck, ask how much became a useful input. Then ask what it cost to get there and what harm it avoided.

That second question prevents a common shortcut. The cheapest private option is not automatically the best environmental option. Some harms may never appear on the operator's bill. But the most expensive option is not automatically greener either. Spending money and using energy to process an unsuitable stream can consume resources that could have done more good somewhere else.

This is why a narrower collection list deserves a real test rather than automatic praise or rejection. Removing a difficult item might improve the rest of the stream. It might also send a useful item to disposal unnecessarily. We need evidence about that item, in that collection system, with those available buyers. The broad argument cannot settle the local decision by itself.

7. Buyers return, and the bill moves

One development in 2018 complicates the picture of China simply abandoning recovered material. In November, ND Paper announced that it had bought a recycled-pulp mill in Fairmont, West Virginia, for sixty-two million dollars. ND Paper was a subsidiary of the Chinese company Nine Dragons. The mill made recycled pulp for paper products. [12]

That announcement does not show that every American recycling problem was solved. A pulp mill is not an outlet for every plastic package. It also does not, by itself, prove that China's restrictions caused this specific purchase. What it does show is that Chinese ownership and American recycling could remain connected through a different stage of production.

There is an important economic distinction between buying a difficult mixed load and buying an industrial input made from it. The second product has had work done to it. Someone has separated material, met a specification, and taken responsibility for the unwanted parts. Changing where that work happens changes who bears those costs.

Investment can help a market adjust, but it takes time and confidence. A buyer considering an expensive mill wants to know that enough suitable material will keep arriving. A town considering better sorting wants to know that a buyer will remain. Each side wants reassurance from the other. Long-term agreements can matter as much as a cheerful campaign asking people to recycle.

Prices also continued to change. In 2021, the Solid Waste Association of North America reported a strong recovery in recycling commodity prices after the earlier downturn. That is an industry association's account of that period. It should not become a promise that prices stay high forever, or that every material had an equally good market. [13]

For a public service, that uncertainty matters. A household expects the truck to come on schedule even when material prices fall. If collection costs are steady while sale revenue moves around, somebody must cover the gap. A program that looks affordable in a strong market may need a different funding plan in a weak one.

Oregon eventually changed part of that plan. Its Recycling Modernization Act began implementation in July 2025. The state's launch announcement described fees paid by producers of packaging, paper, and food-service items to help fund the recycling system. It also described a consistent statewide collection list and requirements related to responsible end markets. [14]

This moved some responsibility toward the companies choosing what enters the waste stream. The intended incentive is straightforward. If a package is costly to manage after use, its maker should face some of that cost when choosing the package. But a program's intended effect is not the same as a measured result. The launch announcement cannot prove that the new system has already solved Oregon's problems.

There is another distinction. The company that sends a payment is not necessarily the person who ultimately bears its cost. Depending on competition and customer demand, some cost might be passed into prices, taken out of profits, or avoided by changing the product. That is a question to investigate, not something the name of the law answers.

8. What the cart can promise

Return to the person in Oregon who put the right things in the recycling cart in 2017. It would be unfair to say that person caused a foreign import restriction. It would also be wrong to say that their effort guaranteed a good result. They were doing one part of a much larger job.

This history suggests starting with the intended use and working backward. Who wants this material? How clean must it be? Who can get it to that standard? What happens when the price falls or that buyer goes away?

Those questions may lead to a simpler collection program. They may lead to investment in better sorting or to a better-designed package. They do not support putting every material into one trash can just because one export market failed. Oregon's bottle returns are a warning against that leap.

They also do not support collecting every package in the hope that someone, somewhere, will eventually figure it out. That pushes the hard choice onto the next person in the chain. It can leave a sorting worker, a town, or a family near a processor to absorb the cost of someone else's reassuring label.

The conclusion here is that a program should make fewer promises if that is what it takes to make honest ones. A shorter list with a verified use can be better than a longer list that counts collection as success. Whether it actually is better still depends on the costs and benefits of the materials being removed.

The lesson reaches beyond recycling. Environmental programs depend on people responding to prices, rules, and the choices available to them. Those responses can cross borders. When the arrangement changes, the physical objects do not disappear. Somebody still has to deal with them.

The arrows in the recycling symbol point back toward each other. In real life, people have to connect them. Oregon's experience shows what happens when one of those connections breaks, and why repairing it takes more than asking households to try harder.
