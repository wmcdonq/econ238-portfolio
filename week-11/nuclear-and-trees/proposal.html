Nuclear Power and New Trees

Walter McDonald | ECON 238 | Week Eleven | November 15, 2026

My idea is to run nuclear plants wherever they make sense and put more money into new forests. I started with a much harsher version: get rid of solar companies and use their research money for trees. That version has a money problem. A company closing does not hand its research budget to the government. It also has a power problem if gas or coal fills the gap.

I propose a national nuclear building program with a first target of 100 gigawatts (GW) of added capacity by 2050. A GW measures a plant's power. The goal would be to repeat a small number of licensed designs at suitable sites, instead of making each project a new experiment. Retiring coal sites and existing nuclear sites should be checked first. Existing reactors would still need safety approval to keep running.

Alongside that program, Congress would create a forest fund of up to $220 million a year, using only solar-program funds it can actually redirect. Any gap would need a separate, openly stated budget decision. The money would restore suitable former forest land and pay for care, not just count seedlings put in the ground. Nuclear construction would have its own financing.

Solar would lose its place only where the replacement works. Before cutting solar output, the utility would have to show that nuclear can cover the needed power, bills are reasonable, and fossil-fuel use will not rise. Even then, retiring a working panel early needs a stronger reason than disliking its industry. Replacing coal or gas usually offers a much larger climate gain.

This is a big bet on steady power and living forests. It would shift the focus of energy policy toward a national reactor buildout and away from assuming that more solar is always the answer. It still has to beat a serious alternative: a mixed grid using nuclear, solar, wind, storage and better power lines.

The nuclear-and-forest combination is a proposal. All figures below are back-of-envelope tests. They show the scale of the idea and where it could fail; they do not prove that every site should use it.

Power and construction

EIA reported a 91.1% capacity factor for U.S. nuclear plants in 2025. That means annual output was 91.1% of what the plants could produce at full power all year. Utility-scale solar PV was 24.0%. I use these historical figures as starting assumptions. A new fleet could perform differently. [1]

One terawatt-hour (TWh) is one billion kilowatt-hours. Equal annual energy does not mean equal service: solar produces at particular hours, while nuclear also needs outages, reserves and a working grid. The roughly 555-GW figure uses a rounded 2025 utility-scale total. It excludes small-scale power, future growth and extra capacity for hourly reliability. It is a scale check, not a complete all-nuclear grid design. [9]

The first 100 GW would equal about 18% of that historical annual output. It would not supply all U.S. electricity, much less every car, furnace and factory. To reach the target, a planning schedule might prepare sites and suppliers in 2027-2030 and commission plants during 2031-2050. That is an ambitious schedule assumption, not a promise.

DOE published the $6,000-$10,000/kW range for first-of-a-kind advanced nuclear projects in 2023. The $14,000 stress case is my assumption. These are older benchmark dollars, not inflation-adjusted 2026 bids. No cost reduction from repeated construction is assumed here. [4]

The capital charge uses a 7% real financing rate and a 60-year life: annual charge = cost x 0.07 / (1 - 1.07^-60). Divide that by annual output to get dollars per MWh. This excludes interest during construction, fuel, staff, maintenance, waste, shutdown work and grid costs. It is not the full electricity price. Nuclear needs patient investors and customers willing to sign long contracts; a research-budget cut cannot pay for it.

Money and forests

DOE's latest budget table lists $220 million enacted for the former Solar Energy Technologies program in FY2026 and a zero-dollar FY2027 request. The request does not prove that the money has been cut or is free to spend elsewhere. Congress would have to approve a transfer, and existing awards may limit what is available. If no funds can be redirected, this source pays for zero trees. [2]

First Solar spent $233.4 million on R&D in 2025, about 4.5% of its $5.219 billion in sales. That does not establish waste, and it is only one company. The spending pays people and buys work; it is not an idle pile of cash. A company ban could destroy useful knowledge and jobs without creating public revenue. My funding plan therefore counts no private R&D money. [3]

For a one-year forest program, assume the full $220 million is lawfully funded. Reserve 20%, or $44 million, for nurseries, monitoring and program support. Spend the other $176 million on sites at an assumed $3,000 per hectare. This is a planning allowance, not a contractor quote. A hectare is about 2.47 acres.

A U.S. reforestation study estimated a weighted planting density of 1,162 seedlings per hectare and planting costs averaging $1,262 per hectare in 2020 dollars, with wide regional variation. Its cost estimate excluded expanding nursery infrastructure. I use a larger round site allowance and a separate reserve, but land purchases are still excluded. Projects would need willing owners or public land; costly land acquisition would reduce the area. [6]

For carbon, use 80% successful establishment as a rough area adjustment and test 3-6 metric tons of CO2 per established hectare per year. That gives about 0.14-0.28 million tons per year for this cohort once growing conditions support those rates. The upper rate comes from a benchmark cited in the study; the lower rate and the 80% factor are assumptions. This is not first-year uptake, a survival guarantee, or saleable carbon credits. Actual growth varies greatly. [6]

The plan needs native species suited to each site, enough seed, trained crews and care after planting. Forest contractors should receive part of their payment after survival checks. Land already recovering without help should not earn a full climate claim. Fire, drought, disease, future logging and lost access can erase gains. Natural grasslands should remain grasslands. Forests offer habitat and water benefits, but their carbon is less secure than fossil carbon left unburned.

Emissions and waiting

Nuclear and solar both have low life-cycle emissions compared with fossil fuels. NREL's 2021 median estimates are 13 grams CO2-equivalent per kWh for light-water nuclear, 43 for solar PV, 486 for natural gas and 1,001 for coal. These include more than smoke at a plant: fuel production, construction and other life-cycle steps count too. They are literature medians, not forecasts for a specific site. [5]

The two displacement rows are separate cases and must not be added. The actual result depends on which plant reduces output at each hour. Transmission limits, demand growth and lower prices could change the result. The comparison makes a useful point: replacing gas has about sixteen times the life-cycle climate benefit of replacing the same annual energy from solar. Existing solar also has much of its construction carbon already spent, so ripping it out can be still less attractive than the simple life-cycle comparison suggests.

A solar ban during nuclear construction is a serious risk. Suppose 10 GW of solar would otherwise be available, but the ban prevents it. At the historical solar capacity factor it would supply 21.02 TWh a year. If gas fills that energy gap for seven years, the extra life-cycle-equivalent emissions are:

21.024 TWh/year x (486 - 43) / 1,000 x 7 years = 65.2 million tons CO2e.

This is a stress test, not a prediction that every blocked panel causes gas generation. It treats life-cycle factors as simple annual equivalents and does not model the timing of construction emissions or storage. Still, the possible penalty is large. It could dwarf the forest fund's carbon benefit. One year of delay for one 1-GW nuclear project also means about 3.77 million tons of forgone life-cycle-equivalent savings if gas runs instead.

For that reason, the phaseout rule matters. Keep useful solar operating while the reactor program grows. Compare a proposed retirement with keeping it and displacing fossil fuel elsewhere. Do not retire solar unless a local grid study and an open cost comparison support that choice. A bad solar project can lose funding; the entire technology does not have to disappear.

Sites, workers and rules

Nuclear expansion and coal-to-nuclear reuse already have serious supporters. DOE screened 157 retired and 237 operating coal sites and found 80% suitable as candidates for smaller advanced reactors. That does not mean 315 approved sites, or room for a 1-GW reactor at each one. Water, geology, grid access and local safety conditions still need study. [7]

The hard part is making many projects work together. One builder may hesitate to hire or buy equipment without future orders. Utilities may hesitate to order until builders prove they can deliver. A shared order for a few repeated designs can help break that loop. It can also lock the country into a bad design. A limited first batch gives the next batch real evidence to work with.

Steel, concrete, large parts, licensed staff, fuel conversion and enrichment all need dependable supply. The 5-GW yearly average hides a difficult ramp-up. A 1-GW-equivalent site might contain several smaller units; the plan does not assume that a new reactor type is already commercially proven. First projects should use designs with credible licensing and delivery plans. Supplier checks and water studies come before construction promises.

Spent fuel remains a real obligation. NRC describes pool and dry-cask storage and the policy of eventual deep geological disposal. An interim storage cask is not a permanent disposal answer. Each project should budget for storage, security and shutdown, and take part in a funded long-term waste plan. Communities should know these costs before accepting a plant. [8]

This is where Chesterton's Fence matters: understand why a rule exists before tearing it down. Safety checks, water limits and public hearings protect people who do not control the project. The plan should speed repeated work and share technical information across similar plants. It should keep independent safety review and meaningful local input. Faster approval is worth little if it creates an avoidable accident or shuts a project down later.

A plant offers power to a large region, while the closest town bears the traffic, land use and risk. That is an external cost. Host agreements should include local tax support, training, emergency planning and an independent way to raise complaints. A payment can help settle a conflict; it cannot make every location suitable. Clear land and water rights make bargaining easier, but many affected people and long-term waste make a simple private bargain difficult.

Forests have a similar problem. Paying for seedlings alone rewards planting even when the trees die. Paying partly for survival changes that incentive. Owners should agree to maintain the land, and monitoring should check that protection here did not merely push clearing somewhere else. Long contracts create costs, so the budget must cover honest administration as well as visible trees.

Who gains, who pays, and when to stop

Nearby residents could gain cleaner air when a fossil plant closes. Nuclear workers, suppliers and forest crews could gain jobs. Coal and gas owners could lose sales. Solar workers and researchers could lose work or grants. Existing nuclear firms might welcome subsidies while resisting competition. None of those reactions alone tells us whether the plan improves public welfare.

Investors would supply much of the reactor capital and recover it through electricity contracts. Ratepayers would pay through bills, and taxpayers would bear any public subsidy or loan loss. Low-income households should not be forced to cover an unlimited overrun. Contracts should state who absorbs delays and cost increases before the first concrete is poured. Public support should be capped, with further spending needing a new decision.

The strongest cost objection is that nuclear might deliver later and cost more than a mixed clean-power plan. The $600 billion low capital case alone is about 2,727 times the $220 million annual forest benchmark. At lower plant output or higher borrowing costs, its cost per MWh rises. The right test is a bid for dependable, low-emission power, including grid and backup costs, against realistic alternatives. The plan should not claim nuclear is cheapest based only on its high capacity factor.

The strongest environmental objection is that cutting solar work can delay useful clean energy, while trees take time and can die. Solar R&D also has knowledge benefits that a private investor may not capture. Removing all public research may therefore save less than it costs society. An independent review should keep work whose expected public benefit is greater than the forest alternative. The fund should receive actual low-value spending reductions, not a promised $220 million regardless of the evidence.

A first batch would cover three to five suitable nuclear sites, with designs and financing settled before large orders. The forest fund would begin on ready sites within its actual appropriation. Each year the public would see cost changes, schedule changes, fossil generation displaced, local complaints, planting survival and spending. Expand the next batch only if results beat the agreed alternatives. Stop a site that fails safety, water or cost tests.

This proposal could appeal to people who want reliable domestic power and people who want lower emissions and restored habitat. It could anger both opponents of nuclear power and supporters of solar research. More innovation is needed in project finance, standard construction, public trust and long-term land care. These jobs are slow and hard to turn into a quick private profit. A short political cycle also rewards an announcement before it rewards a working plant or a healthy forest.

Unknown risks remain. Repeating one design can spread a hidden flaw. Several sites may face the same drought. A large order could raise wages and equipment prices faster than suppliers expand. Cheap power may increase use. Forest contracts could favor large landowners. Separate audits, several suppliers, open bids and staged commitments can reduce these risks without pretending to remove them.

Nuclear power and new trees deserve a large, serious trial. A nationwide solar ban does not pass the same test. The useful version puts nuclear where it can deliver, funds forests with real money, and judges both by results. The goal is more reliable power and less harm at a cost people can defend.
